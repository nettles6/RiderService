How Often Should You Service a Motorcycle?

Motorcycles work their engines harder than most cars do, and they have less margin for neglected maintenance. The right service schedule depends on your bike, how you ride and how old the oil and parts are, not just the odometer.

Start with your owner's manual

The manufacturer's schedule is the baseline. It lists service intervals by distance and by time, and the two work together: you service at whichever comes first. A bike that covers only 2,000 km a year still needs its oil changed, because oil degrades with age and moisture even when the engine is idle.

What most schedules cover

  • Engine oil and oil filter: the most frequent job. Many bikes call for a change somewhere between 5,000 and 10,000 km, or at least once a year. Your manual gives the exact figure.
  • Air filter: inspected regularly and replaced or cleaned at longer intervals, depending on the type.
  • Spark plugs: replaced at longer intervals, which vary a lot with plug type.
  • Brake fluid: absorbs moisture over time, so it is usually replaced on a time basis, commonly every couple of years.
  • Coolant (liquid-cooled bikes): replaced on a time or distance basis.
  • Chain, sprockets and cables: cleaned, lubricated and adjusted often. Chains are a wear item and are replaced when they stretch past their limit.
  • Tyres, brake pads and fasteners: checked at every service and replaced on condition, not on a fixed schedule.
  • Valve clearances: checked at longer intervals on many engines. This is a more involved job.

When to service sooner

Shorten the intervals if the bike spends time in conditions the manual calls severe. Typical examples are:

  • Short trips where the engine rarely reaches full operating temperature
  • Stop-start commuting in heavy traffic
  • Dusty, muddy or sandy roads, or off-road riding
  • Hot weather, towing or consistently carrying a passenger and luggage
  • Track days or regular hard riding

Bikes that sit unused

A bike stored for months should have fresh oil before it is stored and a fuel stabiliser if it will sit for a long time. Check the battery, tyre pressures and brake fluid before riding again. Old oil that has sat in an engine is not a safe shortcut to skip a change.

Keep a service record

Write down the date, odometer reading and work done each time. A record helps you stay on schedule, makes diagnosing faults easier and adds to resale value.
