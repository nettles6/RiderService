Motorcycle Brake Fluid: Types and When to Change It

Brake fluid transfers the force from your hand or foot to the brake calipers. It is easy to forget because it lives sealed in the system, but it ages, and old fluid reduces how well your brakes work. Brakes are a safety system, so if you are unsure about any of this work, have a qualified mechanic do it.

Why it needs replacing

Most brake fluids absorb moisture from the air over time, even in a sealed system. Water in the fluid lowers its boiling point and can cause corrosion inside calipers and master cylinders. Heavy braking creates heat, and fluid that has boiled forms bubbles that compress, which gives a soft lever and reduced braking. For that reason, manufacturers set a time-based interval, commonly every couple of years. Your manual gives the exact figure.

DOT ratings

The DOT number is a standard that sets minimum performance, including boiling points.

  • DOT 3, DOT 4 and DOT 5.1 are glycol-based fluids. Higher numbers generally have higher boiling points, but only use the grade your manual or the reservoir cap specifies.
  • DOT 5 is silicone-based and is not compatible with glycol fluids. Never mix it with DOT 3, 4 or 5.1, and only use it if the system was designed for it.

Handling it safely

  • Brake fluid damages paint and plastics, so protect the tank and bodywork and wipe spills straight away.
  • Use fluid from a fresh, sealed container. An opened bottle absorbs moisture, so do not keep it for long.
  • Keep the reservoir level between its marks, and keep dirt out when the cap is open.
  • Used fluid is hazardous waste. Take it to a council facility or recycling point.

Warning signs

  • A soft, spongy or inconsistent lever or pedal
  • The lever pulling closer to the bar than usual
  • Dark, dirty or cloudy fluid in the reservoir
  • Fluid level dropping quickly, or fluid around calipers or hoses
  • Brakes that fade during long or hard braking

A falling fluid level can also mean worn pads, but a sudden drop or visible leak needs urgent attention. Do not ride a bike with doubtful brakes.

Changing the fluid

Replacing fluid involves flushing the old fluid out through each caliper, a process called bleeding, and it needs care to avoid air in the system. If you are not confident, a workshop can do this quickly, and it is also a good time to have pads and discs checked.
